About the data

Near-surface air temperature projections from CESM2, CanESM5, and MIROC6, averaged equally across the three models. The data covers SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5 from 2015 to 2100. Each model's annual temperature is compared with its own 1995-2014 mean, then averaged across longitude and grouped into 5° latitude bands. Temperature differences have the same numerical value in kelvin and degrees Celsius.

The global averages are area-weighted using the cosine of each band's center latitude. City markers are geographic reference points for band averages. These projections describe broad spatial patterns, and the three-model mean does not display the spread among individual models. Warming is measured against the recent historical baseline shown here.

Sources CMIP6 public dataset · Natural Earth outlines via World Atlas. Maps use the Equal Earth projection.

Prototype notes

What we have done so far

We selected CMIP6 temperature projections and processed three models under three emissions scenarios into annual warming for 36 latitude bands. We checked the exported tables for missing values, duplicate records, complete coverage, and agreement between the ensemble and individual-model averages. We added a global overview chart and two linked world maps with shared time controls and a common color scale. Readers can select a city or latitude band and compare its projected warming with the global average. A linked time-series chart shows the selected band's trajectory, and a supplementary chart compares the bands with the most and least late-century warming. The page includes geographic context, explanatory text, and a description of the data's resolution.

What will be the most challenging part to design and why

The hardest design challenge is making geographic exploration meaningful when our current data represents latitude-band averages. City markers need to help readers orient themselves while clearly communicating the geographic scale of each value. A second challenge is planning out map colors, scenario controls, and line charts so readers can follow a comparison without losing their selected location. Strong polar warming also creates a wide numerical range, so we need a shared color scale that preserves differences across the rest of the planet. We will refine annotations and test whether readers can explain what the global average hides after exploring the page. For the final version, we will decide whether gridded data and model-spread information would improve the story.
